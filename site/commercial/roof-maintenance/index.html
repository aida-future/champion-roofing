Commercial roof maintenance

Low slope roofs fail quietly. Scheduled inspection catches penetrations, drainage and seam problems while they are still repairs rather than replacements.

Free assessment (405) 841-7663
The case for it

A commercial roof rarely fails all at once

It fails at a detail. Water gets in, the assembly beneath gets wet, and the problem spreads outward from that point. By the time it shows on a ceiling tile, it has usually been happening for a while.

That is the whole argument for looking at the roof on a schedule rather than waiting for a phone call from a tenant. Catching a failed penetration detail early is a repair. Finding it two years later is frequently a tear off.

It also keeps restoration on the table. A coating is only viable over a roof that is still sound, so maintenance is what preserves the cheaper option for later.

  • Penetrations and rooftop equipment. The most common origin of a commercial leak.
  • Drainage, drains and scuppers. Ponding water finds every weakness a roof has.
  • Seams, flashings and upstands. Where thermal movement concentrates.
  • Documented each visit. So you have a record of how the roof is changing.

The checklist

What we look at on a maintenance visit

The same list every time, so the record is comparable from one visit to the next.

Penetrations

Every pipe, conduit, curb and unit passing through the membrane. Each one is an individually flashed detail and each one can fail.

Rooftop equipment

The membrane around plant that other contractors service. Foot traffic and dropped tools are a recurring and underestimated source of damage.

Drainage

Drains, scuppers and any evidence of ponding. A low slope roof depends entirely on water leaving it.

Seams and flashings

The joints between membrane sheets and at every upstand. These carry the thermal movement and open up first.

The field

General surface condition, weathering, and anything that has changed since the last visit.

A written record

Findings documented each visit, so you can see how the roof is trending rather than guessing.

Maintenance FAQs

Commercial maintenance questions

For property owners, managers and facilities teams.

Why does a commercial roof need scheduled maintenance?

Because on a commercial building, roof problems stay hidden. On a house a leak announces itself on a ceiling. On a large low slope roof above a suspended ceiling, water can be entering for a long time before anybody notices, and by then it has been finding its way into insulation and decking.

Scheduled inspection catches the small things while they are still small, which is substantially cheaper than reactive repair and far less disruptive than a replacement.

What actually gets checked?

Penetrations, rooftop equipment and the areas around it, drainage including drains and scuppers, seams between membrane sheets, flashings and upstands, and the general condition of the field.

Those are the things that fail on a low slope roof, in roughly that order.

How often should a commercial roof be inspected?

Twice a year is a sensible baseline for most buildings, plus an additional look after any significant storm.

Buildings with a lot of rooftop plant being serviced by other contractors are worth checking more often, because foot traffic and equipment work are a common source of membrane damage.

Do other contractors really damage the roof?

It happens more than building owners expect. HVAC and other equipment gets serviced on the roof over the years, and not every contractor is careful about the membrane they are standing on or the tools they set down.

It is not usually malicious, but it is a genuine and recurring source of damage, and it is one of the better arguments for looking at the roof on a schedule.

Can maintenance extend the life of the roof?

Meaningfully, yes, because most commercial roofs do not fail all at once. They fail at a detail, water gets in, the assembly beneath gets wet, and the problem spreads from there.

Catching that detail early is the difference between a repair and a tear off. It is also what makes a restoration coating a viable option later rather than a wasted spend.
